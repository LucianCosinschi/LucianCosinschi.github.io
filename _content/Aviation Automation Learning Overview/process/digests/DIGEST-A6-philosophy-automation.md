# Digest — A6: The philosophy of automation

Companion to `A6-philosophy-automation.md`.

---

## The intellectual through-line

**The problem is stated once, in 1983, and never really answered.** Bainbridge's *Ironies of Automation* is a process-control paper with flight-deck examples, not an aviation paper. Its logic is a chain of reversals. The designer who thinks the operator unreliable "still leaves the operator to do the tasks which the designer cannot think how to automate," producing "an arbitrary collection of tasks" with no support designed for them. The operator's skills then decay — "physical skills deteriorate when they are not used" — so "a formerly experienced operator who has been monitoring an automated process may now be an inexperienced one." Yet take-over comes precisely when things are worst: the operator "needs to be more rather than less skilled, and less rather than more loaded, than average." Monitoring cannot rescue this, because "the automatic control system has been put in because it can do the job better than the operator, but yet the operator is being asked to monitor that it is working effectively" — and where the machine's decisions exceed human judgement, "the human monitor has been given an impossible task." Her conclusion is a training conclusion: **"it is the most successful automated systems, with rare need for manual intervention, which may need the greatest investment in human operator training."**

**Norman (1990) reframes the target.** The pathology is not the *quantity* of automation but its feedback: automation "at an intermediate level of intelligence, powerful enough to take over control that used to be done by people, but not powerful enough to handle all abnormalities," whose intelligence "is insufficient to provide the continual, appropriate feedback that occurs naturally among human operators." This constrains what training can do — training can teach active interrogation of an under-communicative machine, but cannot manufacture information the machine withholds. His remedy permits *more* autonomy as well as less; citers routinely quote only half.

**Billings systematises the response.** Premise, axiom, corollaries: the human must be in command; to command, be involved; to be involved, be informed; must be able to monitor the automation; automation must therefore be predictable; automation must also monitor the human; every intelligent element must understand the others' intent. Plus: automate only for good reason, and design automation "simple to train, to learn, and to operate."

**Dekker & Woods (2002) make the move that matters.** Substitution-based allocation "cannot provide progress"; quantitative "who does what" fails "because the real effects of automation are qualitative: it transforms human practice and forces people to adapt their skills and routines." The right question is "How do we make them get along together?" Woods (2002) supplies the mechanism: the **Substitution Myth** — the belief that machines can be swapped for people "preserving the system though improving the results" — and the **law of stretched systems**: "every system is stretched to operate at its capacity; as soon as there is some improvement… it will be exploited to achieve a new intensity and tempo of activity." Hollnagel & Woods supply the unit of analysis: the **joint cognitive system**, the ensemble, not human-plus-machine.

**Resilience engineering generalises it, and gets argued with.** Safety-II moves from "as few things as possible go wrong" to "as many things as possible go right," and names training explicitly as part of the Taylorist machinery for forcing work-as-done into work-as-imagined. Leveson — *co-editor of the 2006 founding volume* — later publishes against it. Hollnagel & Dekker (2024) turn the ironies argument on human factors itself, including training.

**Dekker (2003) is the training pivot:** "Discouraging people's attempts at adaptation can increase the number of failures to adapt… Allowing procedural leeway without investing in people's skills at adapting… can increase the number of failed attempts at adaptation." Latitude without invested skill is worse than either extreme.

**Leveson closes the loop with the clearest verbatim statement in the corpus:** "training requirements go up (not down) in automated systems, and they change their nature"; operators need "a model of the automation and its operation"; they need "General strategies rather than specific responses"; and "Knowledge turns unidentifiable weak signals into identifiable strong signals. People need to know what to look for."

## The theory of learning implied

Five commitments run through the tradition:

1. Competence is **maintained, not acquired** — the decay rate sets the recurrency schedule.
2. It is **situated and feedback-dependent**; Bainbridge explicitly rules classroom instruction inadequate.
3. The object is **strategies, not responses** — asserted independently by Bainbridge (1983) and Leveson (2011) in near-identical words.
4. It requires a **second mental model**, of the controller as well as the controlled process.
5. It is **joint**, so it cannot be certified in the individual alone — the trainable skill is coordination with a partner that cannot explain itself.

**The unresolved tension the report must not paper over.** This tradition demands more training (Bainbridge, Baxter, Leveson) while doubting training is the right lever (Woods' stretched systems predicts the margin gets spent; Hollnagel & Dekker 2024 name training as a variability-suppression instrument). The resolution consistent with the sources: "training" is doing two incompatible jobs — procedural transmission, which they all attack, versus capacity to act in unspecified situations, which they all demand.

## Back-attributed coinages found

- **"Bainbridge's four ironies" is a later invention.** She numbers exactly two, both about the *designer*. The rest are "a more serious irony" and "perhaps the final irony." Do not attribute a numbered list of three or four to her. `[VERIFIED by inspection]`
- **Billings did not coin "human-centered automation"** and says so: *"The term is not mine, and I have been unable to find out who first conceived it."* `[VERIFIED]`
- **"The law of stretched systems" — the name is Woods'; the attribution to Hirschhorn resolves to a citation of a quotation.** Woods' own reference reads: "Hirschhorn, L. (1997). Quoted in Cook, R. I., Woods, D. D. and Miller, C. (1998). A Tale of Two Stories…" — no independent Hirschhorn publication. Cite Woods (2002). `[VERIFIED]`

- **Norman (1990) is routinely half-quoted** — his remedy permits *more* autonomy as well as less. `[VERIFIED against abstract]`
- **Resilience engineering is not a consensus.** Leveson co-edited the 2006 founding volume and published against Safety-II in 2020. `[co-editorship VERIFIED]`
